About US Housing Data

US Housing Data is an independent, data-driven resource that provides free housing market information for every U.S. state and county. Our goal is simple: make authoritative housing data accessible, understandable, and useful for homebuyers, renters, researchers, and curious citizens.

What We Cover

We publish county-level and state-level housing metrics for all 50 U.S. states and the District of Columbia, covering more than 3,100 counties:

  • Median Home Value — the midpoint of all owner-occupied housing values
  • Median Gross Rent — the midpoint of monthly rental costs, including utilities
  • Owner Occupancy Rate — the percentage of occupied units that are owner-occupied
  • Price-to-Rent Ratio — a key indicator of whether buying or renting is more favorable
  • Historical Price Trends — multi-year price and rent trajectories
  • FHFA House Price Index — recent market momentum data
  • Local Employment & Wages — county-level jobs and average weekly wages
  • Housing Supply — new residential building permits authorized
  • Migration Patterns — county-to-county population and income flows
  • Mortgage Rates — 30-year and 15-year fixed mortgage rate trends
  • Public Health Indicators — county-level chronic disease prevalence and health behaviors
  • Local Amenities — access to schools, healthcare, groceries, parks, and other everyday services

Why Market Score?

Traditional housing sites show raw metrics — median home value, rent, growth rate — but leave interpretation to the user. This works for analysts but not for most buyers, renters, or curious citizens who need quick, actionable context.

Market Score condenses 9 data sources and 12+ metrics into a single 0–100 rating across four dimensions that matter most to housing decisions:

  • Affordability — Can a typical household afford to buy here?
  • Stability — Is the market predictable, or volatile?
  • Momentum — Is the market growing at a sustainable pace?
  • Vitality — Is the local economy attracting and retaining residents?

The score is not a prediction. It reflects conditions as of the most recent data release. A high score doesn't guarantee outcomes, and a low score doesn't mean a county is unlivable — it means the market presents greater challenges on the measured axes.

How to use it: A buyer prioritizing affordability should weight the Affordability axis higher; an investor focused on growth should look at Momentum and Vitality. The Market Score is a starting point for exploration, not a verdict.

Market Score Methodology

Each U.S. county is assigned an editorial Market Score (0–100) computed from four normalized axes. Scores are relative percentile ranks across all U.S. counties, refreshed quarterly as new data becomes available.

AxisWeightInputsInterpretation
Affordability 30% Price-to-Income Ratio, Rent Burden Higher = more affordable relative to local incomes
Stability 25% Owner Occupancy Rate, Price Volatility Higher = less volatile, more owner-occupied
Momentum 25% 5-Year CAGR, FHFA YoY Change Higher = steady, sustainable price growth
Vitality 20% New Housing Permits, Net Migration, Wages, Health & Amenity Access Higher = active local economy, healthy population, and convenient services

Scores are computed using percentile-rank normalization rather than z-scores, making them robust to outliers. Each axis is scaled 0–100 where 50 represents the national median. The composite score is the weighted average of the four axes.

Tier thresholds: Excellent 80+ · Strong 70–79 · Balanced 60–69 · Challenging 50–59 · Stressed below 50.

Detailed Source Links

  • U.S. Census Bureau — American Community Survey (ACS) 5-Year Estimates
    Provides median home values, median gross rent, and tenure data. Learn more at census.gov.
  • Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) — House Price Index (HPI)
    Provides a quarterly index of single-family home price changes based on millions of mortgage transactions. Learn more at fhfa.gov.
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW)
    Provides county-level employment, wage, and establishment data. Learn more at bls.gov/cew.
  • U.S. Census Bureau — Building Permits Survey
    Provides monthly and annual counts of new residential construction authorized. Learn more at census.gov/construction/bps.
  • IRS Statistics of Income — County-to-County Migration Data
    Provides annual migration flows derived from individual tax returns. Learn more at irs.gov.
  • FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis
    Provides economic time series including national mortgage rate benchmarks. Learn more at fred.stlouisfed.org.
  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — PLACES: Local Data for Better Health
    Provides model-based county-level estimates for chronic disease prevalence, health behaviors, and preventive service use. Learn more at cdc.gov/places.
  • OpenStreetMap — Point-of-Interest Data (via Overpass API)
    Provides geographic data on schools, hospitals, pharmacies, grocery stores, parks, and other local amenities. Learn more at openstreetmap.org. License details at openstreetmap.org/copyright.

Our Methodology

We aggregate raw data from these sources and compute derived metrics (such as price-to-rent ratio and year-over-year changes) using transparent, reproducible formulas. We clearly label the vintage (year or quarter) of all data and never claim to provide real-time prices.

Where county-level data is unavailable (for example, FHFA HPI is limited to areas with sufficient mortgage transaction volume), we indicate this explicitly rather than substituting estimates.
