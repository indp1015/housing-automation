November 2026 U.S. Housing Market Report

Market Update · Published 2026-11-01 · 9 min read

The U.S. housing market in November 2026 presents a landscape defined by stark divergence, where national affordability remains constrained by persistent borrowing costs while regional disparities widen to historic proportions. With the 30-year fixed mortgage rate holding steady at 7.32% as of late October 2026, the cost of entry for prospective buyers remains a significant barrier, yet this macroeconomic headwind has not stalled price appreciation in key segments of the market. Instead, the data reveals a bifurcated economy where luxury enclaves and rural frontiers are experiencing robust growth, driven by distinct demographic and economic forces that operate largely independently of national interest rate trends.

Market Overview: A Tale of Two Markets

The macroeconomic environment for housing in late 2026 is characterized by a tension between high financing costs and resilient demand in specific geographic pockets. The Federal Reserve’s monetary policy, reflected in the 7.32% national mortgage rate, continues to act as a brake on broad-based volume, limiting the pool of qualified buyers and slowing transaction speeds in median-priced markets. However, aggregate price indices suggest that the market has not entered a correction phase. Rather, the housing sector has segmented, with wealthier buyers and those in specific regional economies sustaining price momentum despite the elevated cost of debt.

When analyzing the top ten states by year-over-year (YoY) change according to the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA), a clear pattern emerges: the strongest growth is not concentrated in the traditional coastal hubs that dominated the pre-2020 narrative. Alaska leads the nation with a YoY increase of 8.28%, followed by Vermont at 7.16% and Hawaii at 5.93%. This distribution indicates that demand is being driven by factors other than simple tech-sector employment, including energy sector wages, remote work migration, and limited supply in smaller, high-desirability markets. The presence of states like West Virginia (+5.69%) and Illinois (+5.54%) in the top ten further complicates the narrative, suggesting that affordability-driven demand in lower-cost regions is also contributing to price appreciation, albeit from a much lower base.

This divergence is most visible when comparing the national average to the extremes of the county-level data. While the national mortgage rate of 7.32% makes a $300,000 home significantly more expensive to finance than it was in 2021, the median price in many of the fastest-growing counties remains well below the national average. This suggests that the "housing crisis" is not a uniform experience but a localized one, where the impact of interest rates is felt most acutely in the middle of the market, while the top and bottom ends are propelled by different structural forces.

The Pinnacle of Value: Most Expensive Markets

At the apex of the U.S. housing market, prices have continued to climb, driven by scarcity and the concentration of high-income households. Teton County, Wyoming, stands as the most expensive county in the nation, with a median home price of $1,633,900. This figure represents a 5-year Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 12.0%, the highest among the top five most expensive counties. The sustained appreciation in Teton County reflects its status as a premier destination for luxury real estate, where supply is physically constrained by geography and zoning, and demand is fueled by high-net-worth individuals seeking both recreational access and tax advantages.

Teton WY $1,633,900 12.0%
Nantucket MA $1,593,800 7.0%
San Mateo CA $1,559,600 7.9%
Marin CA $1,507,300 7.5%

Following Teton County, Nantucket, Massachusetts, holds the second position with a median price of $1,593,800. However, its 5-year CAGR of 7.0% is notably lower than Teton’s, suggesting that while prices remain elite, the pace of appreciation has moderated compared to the Wyoming outlier. The California triad of San Mateo, Marin, and Santa Clara counties completes the top five, with median prices ranging from $1,490,600 to $1,559,600. These counties, located in the San Francisco Bay Area, continue to benefit from the high income levels of the technology sector. Santa Clara County, home to Silicon Valley, shows a 5-year CAGR of 8.7%, indicating that despite the national mortgage rate of 7.32%, the demand for housing in this tech-centric region remains robust enough to outpace the broader national trend.

The data highlights a clear distinction between "luxury" and "high-cost." While all five counties exceed the $1.4 million mark, their growth trajectories differ. Teton County’s 12.0% CAGR suggests a market that is decoupling from traditional economic indicators, driven perhaps by capital appreciation expectations and lifestyle factors. In contrast, the California counties, with CAGRs between 7.0% and 8.7%, are growing at a pace that is strong but more aligned with long-term inflation and income growth. This suggests that while the entry price is high, the market is not experiencing the speculative overheating seen in Teton County. The persistence of these high prices, even in a high-interest-rate environment, underscores the inelasticity of supply in these specific geographic locations.

Velocity in the Periphery: Fastest-Growing Markets

While the most expensive markets capture headlines, the most dynamic price action is occurring in some of the least expensive and most remote counties in the United States. The top five fastest-growing counties by 5-year CAGR reveal a trend of significant appreciation in rural and semi-rural areas, driven by factors such as remote work migration, energy sector booms, and the natural appreciation of low-base assets.

  • Jack County, Texas: 17.5% CAGR (Current Value: $251,200)
  • Gem County, Idaho: 16.3% CAGR (Current Value: $428,700)
  • Owyhee County, Idaho: 16.2% CAGR (Current Value: $359,800)
  • Oglala Lakota County, South Dakota: 16.0% CAGR (Current Value: $52,900)
  • De Baca County, New Mexico: 15.7% CAGR (Current Value: $187,500)

Jack County, Texas, leads the nation with a 5-year CAGR of 17.5%. With a current median price of $251,200, this county has seen its value more than double over the five-year period. This growth is likely attributable to its proximity to larger Texas metros and the broader state’s economic expansion, which has drawn both workforce and capital to the periphery. The high CAGR indicates a market that is rapidly transitioning from a low-cost rural area to a more established suburban or exurban destination.

Idaho’s presence in the top three is notable, with both Gem and Owyhee counties posting CAGRs of 16.3% and 16.2%, respectively. These counties, located in the southern and western parts of the state, have benefited from the broader migration trend toward the Pacific Northwest and the appeal of rural living. Gem County’s current median price of $428,700 is significantly higher than Jack County’s, suggesting that while both are growing rapidly, Gem County has already established a higher baseline value, likely due to its proximity to Boise and its recreational amenities. Owyhee County, with a median price of $359,800, follows a similar trajectory, indicating that the demand for rural Idaho real estate is not limited to a single corridor but is spread across the state’s less populated regions.

The inclusion of Oglala Lakota County, South Dakota, in the top five is a striking data point. With a 5-year CAGR of 16.0% and a current median price of just $52,900, this county represents the extreme low end of the market. The high percentage growth from such a low base is mathematically easier to achieve, but it still signals a significant shift in value perception. This could be driven by specific local factors, such as land use changes, resource extraction, or a niche market for affordable land. Similarly, De Baca County, New Mexico, with a 15.7% CAGR and a median price of $187,500, shows that this trend of rapid appreciation in low-cost rural areas is not isolated to a single state or region.

These five counties collectively illustrate that the housing market’s growth engine is not solely powered by high-income urban centers. The combination of remote work flexibility, the search for lower cost of living, and the natural appreciation of undervalued assets has created a secondary market of high-growth opportunities. For data analysts, these markets represent a divergence from the national norm, where a 7.32% mortgage rate might typically suppress growth. Instead, the low absolute prices in these counties mean that even with high interest rates, the monthly payment burden remains manageable for a wider segment of the population, sustaining demand and driving prices higher.

The Floor of the Market: Most Affordable Zones

At the opposite end of the spectrum from Teton County, the most affordable counties in the United States offer median prices that are a fraction of the national average. These markets, predominantly located in the rural South and Midwest, continue to serve as the primary entry point for first-time buyers and those seeking to minimize housing costs.

  • Stonewall County, Texas: $48,600
  • McDowell County, West Virginia: $50,000
  • Oglala Lakota County, South Dakota: $52,900
  • Cochran County, Texas: $53,200
  • Todd County, South Dakota: $53,900

Stonewall County, Texas, holds the distinction of being the most affordable county in the nation, with a median home price of $48,600. This figure is less than 3% of the median price in Teton County, Wyoming. McDowell County, West Virginia, follows closely at $50,000, reflecting the economic challenges and limited demand in parts of Appalachia. The presence of two Texas counties and two South Dakota counties in the top five most affordable list highlights the geographic concentration of low-price housing in these regions.

Oglala Lakota County, South Dakota, appears in both the most affordable and fastest-growing lists, with a median price of $52,900 and a 5-year CAGR of 16.0%. This dual classification is significant, as it indicates that while the absolute price remains very low, the rate of appreciation is high. This suggests that the market is undergoing a structural change, perhaps due to increased interest from remote workers or local economic development, even as it remains one of the cheapest places to buy a home in the country.

The affordability of these counties is a key factor in their demand. With a median price of $50,000, the monthly mortgage payment on a 30-year fixed loan at 7.32% is approximately $420, excluding taxes and insurance. This is significantly lower than the payment on a national median home, making these markets accessible to buyers who are priced out of higher-cost regions. However, the low prices also reflect lower local incomes and limited employment opportunities, which can constrain the long-term sustainability of price growth. Despite this, the data shows that these markets are not stagnant; they are experiencing growth, albeit from a very low base.

The Federal Housing Finance Agency’s (FHFA) year-over-year data provides a broader view of market momentum, revealing which states are leading in price appreciation. The top ten states by YoY change offer insights into the regional drivers of the housing market in 2026.

Alaska leads the nation with a YoY increase of 8.28%, a figure that is significantly higher than the national average. This strong performance is likely driven by the energy sector’s continued strength and the limited supply of housing in the state. Vermont follows with a 7.16% increase, reflecting the state’s appeal to remote workers and its high quality of life. Hawaii, with a 5.93% increase, continues to see strong demand despite its high cost of living, driven by its unique geographic and cultural appeal.

The inclusion of West Virginia (+5.69%) and Illinois (+5.54%) in the top five is noteworthy. West Virginia’s growth, despite its low median prices, suggests a recovery in demand, possibly driven by affordability. Illinois, a large and diverse state, shows strong momentum, likely driven by its major urban centers and the surrounding suburbs. North Dakota (+4.82%) and Wisconsin (+4.76%) round out the top seven, indicating that the Midwest is also experiencing significant price appreciation.

The bottom three states in this top ten list—Connecticut, Rhode Island, and New Jersey—all show YoY changes between 4.53% and 4.59%. These states, located in the Northeast, continue to see steady growth, driven by their proximity to New York City and their strong job markets. The consistency of these figures suggests that the Northeast remains a stable and growing region for housing, even in a high-interest-rate environment.

Outlook: Structural Shifts in the Data

The November 2026 data paints a picture of a housing market that is increasingly defined by geographic and economic segmentation. The national mortgage rate of 7.32% continues to act as a constraint on broad-based demand, but it has not prevented significant price appreciation in both the highest and lowest price tiers. The most expensive markets, led by Teton County, Wyoming, and the San Francisco Bay Area, continue to see strong growth, driven by scarcity and high-income demand. Simultaneously, the most affordable markets, led by Stonewall County, Texas, and McDowell County, West Virginia, are experiencing rapid appreciation, driven by affordability and remote work migration.

The state-level data further supports this view of a bifurcated market. States like Alaska and Vermont are leading in YoY growth, while traditional coastal hubs like New Jersey and Connecticut are maintaining steady but more moderate increases. This suggests that the housing market is no longer driven by a single national trend but by a complex interplay of local factors, including supply constraints, economic development, and demographic shifts.

As the market moves forward, the data suggests that the divergence between high-cost and low-cost markets will likely persist. The high mortgage rate will continue to limit the pool of qualified buyers in median-priced markets, but it will have less impact on the luxury and ultra-affordable segments. For data analysts and market participants, the key takeaway is that the housing market is not a monolith; it is a collection of distinct regional markets, each with its own dynamics and drivers. Understanding these local nuances is essential for making informed decisions in a complex and evolving landscape.

In conclusion, the U.S. housing market in November 2026 is characterized by resilience and divergence. While the national mortgage rate of 7.32% poses challenges for many buyers, the data shows that price appreciation is continuing in both the top and bottom ends of the market. The most expensive counties are seeing double-digit CAGRs in some cases, while the most affordable counties are experiencing rapid growth from a low base. This dual trend suggests that the housing market is adapting to the current economic environment, with demand shifting to regions and price points that remain accessible or desirable despite the high cost of borrowing. The future of the market will likely be shaped by these structural shifts, as buyers and sellers navigate a landscape defined by geographic and economic disparity.
